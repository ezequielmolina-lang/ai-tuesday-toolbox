From Prompts
to Tools

Two years of prompts. Time for the next step:
three ready-to-use tools for the pains you feel every week.

AI Tuesday • World Bank Group • September 2026

A prompt is a great conversation. But on Monday morning the conversation is gone, the answer is buried in a chat from last Tuesday, and you're retyping "go through my emails and tell me what I owe people" for the fortieth time.

Since July 2024 this newsletter has given you prompts. Prompts for ISRs, for missions, for briefs, for ministers with nicknames for your project. They work. They also have a flaw: a prompt gives you an answer, and then you're on your own with it.

A tool is different. A tool remembers. It sorts. It counts what's overdue. It sits on your desktop with the prompt already inside it, and it's there next Monday too.

The news of 2026 is that AI can now build these tools from a plain-English description, so we did. Below are three, made for the World Bank week. They are finished, not demos. Open, use, done. No install, no account, no IT ticket, and nothing you type in them leaves your computer.

Tool 1 · the pain: Monday inbox

My Week

One file, three tabs, and Copilot does the reading.

  • I owe: every action item people are expecting from you in the last 7 days of email and Teams, sorted into overdue, today, this week.
  • Waiting on others: everything you asked for and never got back, with how many days you've been waiting and a Nudge button that opens a polite, ready-to-send reminder in Outlook.
  • Meeting prep: for each meeting in the coming week, what you need to bring (slides, comments, a decision, the pre-read), due the day before.

How it works (the Monday ritual, 10 minutes)

  1. Open the file. Each tab has a "Copy prompt" button.
  2. Paste the prompt into Copilot (work mode), the one that can see your mailbox and calendar. It returns a table.
  3. Paste the table back into the tool. Your week appears, sorted. Tick things off during the week. Next Monday, paste again: it only adds what's new.
One of the three prompts inside (the "waiting on others" one)

Go through my SENT emails and Teams messages from the last 21 days. Find every request I made to someone (a document, comments, an approval, an answer, a decision) that has NOT received a reply yet. Return ONLY a table with columns: What I asked for | Person | Date I asked | Email subject.

Before

Scrolling Sent Items to remember who never answered about the procurement plan. Writing the reminder from scratch. Forgetting the pre-read until 8:55.

After

"Waiting 12 days" in red, one click, reminder drafted with the subject line and the date you asked. Prep items appear the day before the meeting.

Tool 2 · the pain: "does 9am work for everyone?"

Meeting Time Finder

Every team with a country office knows the thread: nine emails to discover that 9am in DC is 7:30pm in Delhi and 6am in Lima, and that the one slot that worked was Tuesday, which is now gone.

Pick the cities on the call (90 to choose from, presets for LAC and global). The grid shows every half hour of the day in each city's local time, green where everyone is inside working hours. It proposes the three best slots and, with one click, copies a line for the invite with every city's local time. Daylight saving is handled automatically, which is more than can be said for most of us.

No AI in this one. Not everything needs a chatbot. Some things need a grid with the right colours.

Tool 3 · the pain: the post-lunch slot of every training

Workshop Quiz

You know the moment. Slide 47, 2:30pm, the room is quiet, the phones are out. Kahoot works, but it needs accounts, wifi that holds, and everyone's phone. This runs from your laptop on the projector: big question, four coloured answers, 30-second timer, reveal, team scoreboard, podium. Participants shout, you click.

Questions come from AI in one step: the tool includes a prompt ("write 8 quiz questions about [your topic] in exactly this format"). Paste Copilot's answer, add your teams, press Start. Works for counterpart workshops, team retreats, onboarding, and settling arguments about who really knows the procurement framework.

Want it different? Ask. Don't code.

This is the part that changes how you think about tools.

You don't need to understand these files. To change anything, drag the file into Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Copilot and say what you want in plain English. It gives you back a new file. That's it. Things that work first time:

  • "Make it Spanish."
  • "In My Week, add a fourth tab called 'Promised to counterparts'."
  • "In the time finder, show a whole week and exclude Friday afternoons."
  • "In the quiz, play a sound at zero and let each team answer on their phones."
  • "Make it an icon on my phone's home screen."

And if the tool you need doesn't exist: "Build a single self-contained HTML file that [does X]. Inputs are [...]. It should show [...]. Save everything in the browser. Works on a phone." Ten minutes later you have version 1. Reply to this email with what you built; the good ones go in the Toolbox with your name on them.

Why this is safe: the tool files contain no data. They're empty shells, so they're fine to share, and fine to remix in any chatbot. Your real data goes in afterwards, in your own browser, and stays there. The reading of your inbox and calendar happens in Copilot, the Bank-approved tool. Never paste internal or "Official Use Only" content into a non-Bank chatbot.

The reality check

Copilot's reading is good, not perfect. It will occasionally miss an implicit ask or invent a due date. Skim the table before you paste it. Thirty seconds, and still faster than reading 140 emails.

These tools live on your computer. What you tick off on your laptop won't show on your phone. That's the price of nothing leaving your machine. If you need a shared team version, that's a Planner or a SharePoint list, and an ITS conversation.
